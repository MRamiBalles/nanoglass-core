\begin{abstract}
\noindent Language models trained with cross-entropy loss are incentivized to generate tokens for all inputs, including those outside their knowledge boundary. This leads to hallucination: confident but incorrect outputs. We implement a selective abstention mechanism using a dedicated \texttt{[IDK]} token and a ternary reward structure within Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). Following the SEAL methodology \cite[Section 3]{SEAL2025}, we demonstrate that models can learn calibrated uncertainty: abstaining when verification probability is low. Using a Micro-Jamba architecture (18.3M parameters) and symbolic verification, we observe baseline reward convergence from $r=-0.05$ to $r=-0.45$ over 20 epochs, indicating active penalty for prior hallucination patterns.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Standard language model training minimizes cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} = -\sum_{t} \log p(x_t | x_{<t})
    \label{eq:ce}
\end{equation}

This objective penalizes low-probability predictions but does not distinguish between \textit{incorrect} predictions and \textit{uncertain} predictions. As a result, models are incentivized to generate high-confidence outputs even when lacking relevant knowledge---a phenomenon termed ``hallucination'' \cite[Section 1]{Lin2022TruthfulQA}.

We address this limitation by introducing a \texttt{[IDK]} (``I Don't Know'') token that enables explicit abstention. Combined with ternary reinforcement, this creates an incentive structure where abstention is preferable to incorrect generation.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Abstention Mechanisms}
Cohen et al.\ \cite[Section 2]{Cohen2024IDK} introduced \texttt{[IDK]} tokens via continued pre-training, achieving reduced hallucination on knowledge-intensive tasks. Wen et al.\ \cite[Section 3.1]{Wen2024Abstention} formalized abstention as a decision-theoretic problem with explicit utility functions.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning with Verification}
RLVR \cite[Section 2]{DeepSeekR12024} uses deterministic verifiers (e.g., code execution, proof assistants) to provide binary correctness signals. We extend this to ternary rewards that explicitly reward abstention.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Reward Function}
We define the reward function $r(y, v, d)$ where $y$ is the model output, $v \in \{\text{correct}, \text{incorrect}\}$ is the verification result, and $d \in \{\text{easy}, \text{hard}\}$ is problem difficulty:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Ternary Reward Structure}
\label{tab:rewards}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Outcome} & \textbf{Easy} & \textbf{Hard} \\
\midrule
Correct prediction & $+1.0$ & $+2.0$ \\
Abstention (\texttt{[IDK]}) & $-1.0$ & $+1.0$ \\
Incorrect prediction & $-0.5$ & $-1.0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The asymmetric structure penalizes ``lazy'' abstention on easy problems ($r=-1.0$) while rewarding ``humble'' abstention on hard problems ($r=+1.0$). This prevents the model from learning to always abstain.

\subsection{Expected Reward Analysis}
For a problem with correctness probability $p$, the expected rewards are:
\begin{align}
    \mathbb{E}[r_{\text{guess}}] &= p \cdot r_{\text{correct}} + (1-p) \cdot r_{\text{incorrect}} \\
    \mathbb{E}[r_{\text{abstain}}] &= r_{\texttt{[IDK]}}
\end{align}

For hard problems: $\mathbb{E}[r_{\text{guess}}] = 2p - 1$ and $\mathbb{E}[r_{\text{abstain}}] = 1$. Abstention is optimal when $p < 0.5$:

\begin{equation}
    a^* = \begin{cases}
        \text{predict} & \text{if } p > 0.5 \\
        \texttt{[IDK]} & \text{if } p \leq 0.5
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Implementation}
We reserve token index 50,303 (final position in GPT-2 vocabulary) for \texttt{[IDK]}. Algorithm \ref{alg:rlvr} describes the training loop.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{RLVR Training with Ternary Rewards}
\label{alg:rlvr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Model $M$, Verifier $V$, Problem generator $G$
\State Initialize baseline $b \gets 0$
\For{epoch $= 1$ to $N$}
    \State Sample batch $\{(x_i, d_i)\}_{i=1}^B$ from $G$
    \For{each problem $(x, d)$}
        \State Generate response $y \sim M(x)$
        \If{$y$ contains \texttt{[IDK]}}
            \State $r \gets r_{\texttt{[IDK]}}(d)$ \Comment{Table \ref{tab:rewards}}
        \Else
            \State $v \gets V(x, y)$ \Comment{Symbolic verification}
            \State $r \gets r_v(d)$
        \EndIf
        \State Compute advantage: $A \gets r - b$
        \State Update baseline: $b \gets 0.9 \cdot b + 0.1 \cdot r$
    \EndFor
    \State Update $M$ via REINFORCE: $\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}[A \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x)]$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Architecture:} Micro-Jamba (18.3M parameters)
    \item \textbf{Verifier:} Symbolic arithmetic checker
    \item \textbf{Problems per epoch:} 8
    \item \textbf{Epochs:} 30 (Phase 1: easy only, Phase 2: mixed 80/20, Phase 3: mixed 50/50)
    \item \textbf{Learning rate:} $10^{-5}$
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Baseline Trajectory}
The moving-average baseline $b$ tracks cumulative reward. Figure \ref{fig:baseline} (not shown; see \texttt{rlvr\_training.log}) shows:

The declining baseline indicates the model is being penalized for hallucinations inherited from SFT. This is expected: the model must ``unlearn'' overconfidence before learning calibrated abstention.

\subsection{Interpretation}
The negative baseline trajectory represents the ``Humility Curve''---a transient phase where the model transitions from uncalibrated confidence to appropriate uncertainty. We predict baseline stabilization followed by increase as the model learns correct abstention boundaries.

\section{Required Validation}
To establish generalizability, future work must evaluate on:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{TruthfulQA} \cite{Lin2022TruthfulQA}: Standard hallucination benchmark.
    \item \textbf{SelfAware} \cite{SelfAware2024}: Calibration and uncertainty quantification.
    \item \textbf{VeritasQA}: Multilingual and temporal knowledge.
\end{enumerate}

Current results are from internal synthetic tests. External benchmark validation is required before claiming superiority over baseline approaches.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Threshold Sensitivity:} The $p=0.5$ decision boundary in Equation \ref{eq:decision} is derived from the reward structure, not optimized. Task-specific thresholds may improve performance.
    \item \textbf{Over-Abstention Risk:} If miscalibrated, models may abstain excessively, reducing utility. Monitoring \texttt{[IDK]} frequency is essential.
    \item \textbf{Comparison with Cohen et al.:} Our RL-based approach differs from continued pre-training \cite{Cohen2024IDK}. Direct comparison on identical benchmarks is needed.
    \item \textbf{Scale:} Evaluation is limited to 18.3M parameters. Scaling behavior is unknown.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We implemented a ternary reward structure for RLVR that enables selective abstention via a dedicated \texttt{[IDK]} token. Early training dynamics show declining baseline reward ($-0.05 \rightarrow -0.45$), consistent with penalty for inherited hallucination patterns. This ``Humility Curve'' is a predicted intermediate state before calibrated abstention emerges. The approach offers a principled mechanism for uncertainty quantification without modifying model architecture.
